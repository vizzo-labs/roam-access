At a glance

{{crossings}} crossings with no kerb or tactile paving info

{{entrances}} venues whose entrance is missing or incomplete

{{steps}} + {{slopes}} flights of steps and slope checkpoints

{{person_hours}} h person-hours: 6 pairs for about {{pair_hours}} hours each

The plan: 12 people in 6 pairs, each pair with at least one person with lived experience of disability. Each pair takes one zone, starts and ends at a step-free meeting point, and walks a loop of 1–3 km over about three hours with rests. On the way they record kerbs, entrances, steps and slopes in OpenStreetMap, and mark step-free entrances and arrival steps in Roam Access.

With fewer people (5–6, as the routing spike assumed): run 3 pairs on the three flat zones with the most venues (C, D, E), and do zones A, B and F on a second morning. Or keep all six zones and do only crossings and entrances, which matter most for routing, and leave slopes and footpaths for later.

Why the estimate is higher than the spike’s 20–29 hours. This list comes from fresh data, so it counts every gap (including the {{crossing_nodes}} crossings that exist only as a point on the road, with no crossing path drawn). It also adds Roam Access entrance pins, photos and arrival steps at each venue, and a slower walking pace (2.5 km/h) for mixed-ability pairs with rests.

Why this day

Roam Access wants to show a step-free walking route to a venue’s accessible entrance. The routing spike (draft PR #18, docs/research/step-free-routing-spike.md) found that the routing engine works, but a route is only as good as OpenStreetMap. In the town centre today:

  • Kerbs: only {{kerb_both}}% of the {{crossing_ways}} crossing paths say what the kerb is like at both ends. For a wheelchair user, a kerb without a ramp is the most common barrier on an otherwise flat route.
  • Slope: almost no footpath has a mapped slope (incline). The engine guesses slopes from a coarse elevation model and misses short, steep bits.
  • Entrances: {{entrance_pct}}% of the {{venues}} venues have an entrance point near them, and none of those says whether it is step-free. Without one, a route ends at the middle of the building or the street.
  • Steps: mapped well, but most flights don’t say how many steps, whether there’s a handrail or whether there’s a ramp.

One well-organised day fixes most of this for the town centre. The data goes into OpenStreetMap, where everyone can use it, including Roam Access’s own routing after its next data refresh.

The task list and the six zones

The list comes from a fresh OpenStreetMap download (scripts/fetch_osm.py, then scripts/build_tasks.py). It includes:

  • every crossing in the town centre without kerb type at both ends or without tactile paving ({{crossings}}, of which {{crossing_nodes}} are only a point on the road; their kerbs go on the sheet and the data lead adds them);
  • every venue without an entrance point ({{entrances_new}}), and every venue whose mapped entrance lacks wheelchair, door, automatic_door or width;
  • slope checkpoints every 120 m on the corridors the spike named (The Corso, Belgrave St, Sydney Rd, East and West Esplanade, Darley Rd), plus footpaths the elevation model rated 8% or steeper;
  • every flight of steps missing step_count, handrail or ramp;
  • {{path_km}} km of footpaths missing surface, smoothness or width. These are lines on the maps, not numbered points: pairs note them as they pass.

The points are split into six zones of similar effort. Each zone has a meeting point and a walking order that starts and ends there.

Meeting points must be checked first. They are flat public spaces in OpenStreetMap, but nobody has checked them on the ground for this day. Walk all six on the recce (see Running the day) with a wheelchair user, and move any that don’t work.

How the hours are worked out: 5 minutes per crossing, 7 per new entrance (including the Roam Access pin, photo and arrival steps), 5 per entrance check, 4 per slope checkpoint, 3 per flight of steps, 1 per 100 m of footpath to note, walking at 2.5 km/h with 35% added for street layout, then 25% for editing and upload. They are estimates; the recce will tell.

Field guide: what to record and how

Print pages: this section is two A4 pages

The golden rule: only record what you see on the ground today. Never copy from Google Maps, Street View, Apple Maps, real-estate photos or any other map. Their data is copyright and can’t go into OpenStreetMap. If you can’t see it or measure it, leave it out.

How to measure

Kerbs and doors: tape measure

  • Kerb lip: stand on the footpath. Put the tape’s end on the road surface at the bottom of the kerb ramp and read the height of the lip in centimetres. Measure in the middle of the ramp, where a wheel would go.
  • Door width: the clear opening with the door fully open, from the door face to the stop on the other side. Not the frame.
  • Footpath width: at the narrowest point you pass, between fixed obstacles.
  • Write centimetres on the sheet; the data lead converts to metres.

Slope: a phone and a ruler

  • iPhone: the built-in Measure app (in Utilities) → Level. It shows degrees.
  • Android: Clinometer + bubble level by plaincode (free, with ads), or the open-source Clinometer by BasicAirData. Calibrate it on a flat table first.
  • Lay a ruler or a short spirit level along the footpath and put the phone on it, lengthways in the direction of travel. This evens out bumpy pavers.
  • Measure the steepest part you’d use, in 2–3 places. Write the highest steady reading and which way is uphill.
  • Phone readings are approximate. That is fine: we record what people will meet, not a survey for building approval.
Degrees to percent (percent = tan(degrees) × 100). Australian Standard AS 1428.1 limits, as reported by secondary sources (the standard itself is paywalled).

Photos

  • No faces, no number plates, no children, no one’s home through a window. Wait until people pass, or point the camera down. If someone is in the shot, take it again.
  • Photograph the thing: the kerb from the footpath, the door from straight on, the steps from the bottom.
  • Ask before photographing inside a shop or café. If staff say no, don’t.
  • Photos for Roam Access go through the app, which removes location data before upload. Photos you take for the data lead go into the shared folder and are deleted 30 days after the day.

Which app

Everyone: StreetComplete (Android and iPhone)

It asks one simple question at a time about what’s around you, and can’t write a wrong tag. It asks about kerb type at crossings, tactile paving (switched on for Australia), steps (count, handrail, ramp, which way is up) and footpath surface. Turn on the smoothness question in Settings before the day.

Free. Version 64 (October 2026) is the first stable iPhone release, so iPhone users should also install the fallback below.

Entrances: Every Door (Android and iPhone)

Its Entrances mode puts an entrance on the building outline and asks the entrance type and wheelchair. Write door type, automatic door and width on the sheet.

Experienced pairs can use MapComplete → OnWheels in the phone’s browser (mapcomplete.org/onwheels), which also asks door type, automatic door and width. It needs mobile data.

  • On paper (the zone sheet): slopes, kerb heights in centimetres, door and footpath widths, and the crossings that are only a point on the road. No beginner app asks these well. The data lead enters them in the iD editor in the week after, and checks the direction of each slope.
  • Fallbacks: Go Map!! (iPhone) and Vespucci (Android) can edit any tag, but they are full editors for experienced mappers.
  • Hashtag: in StreetComplete and Every Door you can’t always type a changeset comment. The data lead adds #MapManly2026 to paper-based edits; app edits are counted by account and time window instead.

Your OpenStreetMap account

  1. At home, go to openstreetmap.org → Sign Up. Use your email and a password, or sign in with Google, Apple, Microsoft, Facebook, GitHub or Wikipedia.
  2. Choose a display name. It is public and shows on every edit. You don’t have to use your real name, but it must not be misleading. You can change it later.
  3. Open the email “Welcome to OpenStreetMap” and click the link. Your account only works after this. No email? Use “Resend the confirmation email”.
  4. Open StreetComplete and Every Door and log in with that account. Send the display name to the organiser if you’re happy to be listed on the event’s wiki page (optional).

Your own account, always. Everyone edits as themselves; no shared accounts. OpenStreetMap’s terms say you must be at least 16 to register yourself; people aged 13–15 can take part only through an account a parent or adult sets up.

Roam Access on the same walk

At every venue on your sheet, also add what you found in Roam Access. It takes two minutes and helps the next person straight away, before any map refresh. These features shipped in PRs #17 (step-free entrance pin) and #19 (step-by-step arrival).

Mark the step-free entrance

  1. Open the place in Roam Access. Under Getting there, tap Mark the entrance (or Share your experience).
  2. In Tell us more, answer Step-free way in?
    • Yes: tap Show where the step-free entrance is.
    • No: say if there is another way in (side, back, lift) and mark that one.
  3. Choose Which door is it?: Main entrance, At the side, At the back, Via a lift or Another door.
  4. Put the pin on the door: drag it, tap the map, use the arrow buttons, or Use my current location while standing at the door. No map? Describe where it is in words.
  5. Add a photo of this entrance and describe it (“Side door with a ramp and a handrail”).
  6. Tap Send it.

If someone already marked it, answer Still the way in? with Yes or Changed.

Add the arrival steps

  1. On the same place, find Step by step from the street and tap Add the first steps (or Add your steps).
  2. Where I started: e.g. “The Corso” or “The bus stop on Belgrave St”.
  3. Write short steps in order, one thing per step. Say what you see: kerbs, ramps, doors, lifts. Add a step for each one.
  4. Add a photo of the way in if it helps, and describe it.
  5. Tap Share my steps.

If steps are already there and they worked for you, tap This worked for me.

Honesty rules still apply. Only record what you experienced today. Your entries show your lived experience (“Wheelchair user”), never your name. Entrances and arrival steps help people find the way in; they never change a place’s verdict. Only community reviews do that.

Running the day

Agenda (a Saturday morning)

8:45
Organisers set up at the base: welcome table, quiet corner, water, printed sheets, chargers.
9:15
Arrive. Help desk: OpenStreetMap account, app installed, join the mapping day in Roam Access, needs set.
9:30
Welcome and Acknowledgement of Country. Briefing (15 min): why, the golden rule, safety, photos, the zone sheets.
9:45
Practice together outside the base (15 min): one kerb, one door, one slope reading, one Roam Access entrance pin.
10:00
Pairs leave for their zones. Mapping, about 3 hours. Rest at least every 45 minutes. Message the base at 11:00 and 12:00.
13:00
Back at the base. Upload anything still on phones; hand sheets and photos to the data lead. Lunch.
13:45
Wrap-up (20 min): what we found, the Roam Access leaderboard on screen, first coverage numbers, thank you.
14:15
Close. In the next week the data lead enters paper notes, draws the crossings that are only points, and reviews every change.

Pairs

  • Every pair includes someone with lived experience of disability where we can: a wheelchair or scooter user, someone who uses a walking aid, someone who is blind or has low vision, or someone with another access need. They notice what others miss, and their reviews in Roam Access carry their lived experience.
  • Pair people who’ve mapped before with people who haven’t. Students and OSM mappers make good partners for disabled community members who know the area.
  • Match zones to people: flat zones (C, D, E) suit wheelchair and scooter users; hilly zones (A, F) suit walkers. Let each pair choose; nobody has to explain why.
  • Support workers come free and are not counted as mappers.

Before the day

  • Four weeks before: post the draft wiki page and the note to the OpenStreetMap Australia community (drafts below), and open sign-ups.
  • Three weeks before: book the base, and Auslan or captions if anyone asks. Order any materials.
  • Two weeks before: recce walk with a wheelchair user: check all six meeting points, the base, the accessible toilet, shade and rest stops on each loop. Re-run build_tasks.py if OpenStreetMap has changed.
  • One week before: send each pair their zone sheet and the field guide, and ask everyone to make their OpenStreetMap account and install the app at home.
  • Two days before: check the weather and the heat forecast; confirm or move the day.
  • The day before: print the sheets (one per pair plus spares) and charge everything.

An accessible event

The day itself has to work for the people we’re mapping for.

The base

  • Step-free entry, checked on the recce, not just listed.
  • An accessible toilet on site or within 100 m on a step-free path.
  • Shade and seats, inside and out; a quiet space anyone can use without explaining why.
  • Close to the ferry and to accessible bus stops; accessible parking and a drop-off point nearby.
  • Water refill, and a fridge or esky for medication.

On the loops

  • Rest stops with shade and seats at least every 45 minutes; the recce marks them on the sheets.
  • Every pair carries water and a phone with the base’s number saved.
  • Anyone can stop at any time. A half-done zone is fine.

Communication

  • Auslan interpreters or live captions on request, for the briefing and the wrap-up. Ask at sign-up; book early.
  • Large-print (18 pt) and digital copies of the field guide and sheets; a plain-text version for screen readers.
  • Briefing said, written and shown. Face the group; use a microphone if there is one.
  • Easy Read version of the invitation on request.

At sign-up, ask only

  • “What do you need to take part?” (interpreter, captions, support worker, large print, quiet space, food needs, a flat zone).
  • Never ask for a diagnosis. Keep the answers private and delete them after the day.

Safety, materials and budget

Safety

At crossings

  • Never stand on the road to measure. Measure kerbs from the footpath, at the edge, when no cars are turning.
  • One person measures, the other watches traffic and bikes.
  • Cross only with the lights or when it’s clear, like anyone else. Don’t press crossing buttons just to test them.
  • The harbour-front path and The Corso are shared with bikes and scooters: keep to one side.
  • Don’t block doorways, kerb ramps or footpaths while you work.

Heat and sun

  • In Sydney from November the UV index is usually extreme by mid-morning. Hats, sunscreen (SPF 50+), sunglasses, long sleeves; reapply every 2 hours.
  • Drink water at every rest stop. Wheelchair users and some people with disability are more at risk in heat.
  • If the Bureau of Meteorology forecasts 32 °C or more, or a heatwave warning, move the day or finish by 11:30.
  • Start and end check-ins at the base; a message at 11:00 and 12:00. If a pair doesn’t answer within 15 minutes, call them.

Private property: survey from public places only. To measure a door, ask staff first; if they say no, leave it and note “not measured”. Be friendly: “We’re mapping step-free access for an open community map.”

Materials

  • Zone sheets, one per pair plus spares, on clipboards; pencils
  • 6 tape measures (5 m, metric) and 6 short rulers or 30 cm spirit levels for slopes
  • 6 power banks and cables
  • Sunscreen, spare hats, water bottles and a refill station
  • First-aid kit, including a heat pack and cold packs
  • Large-print copies of the field guide
  • Name stickers (first name only, optional)
  • The Roam Access join poster (from the app’s organiser kit) and a screen for the leaderboard
  • Optional: hi-vis vests, so people can see you near crossings
  • Lunch, with options for food needs

OpenStreetMap community rules

The OpenStreetMap Foundation’s Organised Editing Guidelines cover coordinated editing by organisations. They say they are not meant for “mapping parties between friends”, but a public event run by a company for its product is close enough that we follow them in full. It’s also good manners: local mappers will see a burst of edits in Manly.

What we do

  • A wiki page at Organised Editing/Activities/Map Manly 2026, listed on Organised Editing/Activities, before we announce the day (draft below).
  • Tell the community at least two weeks before, in a public, archived place: the Oceania category of the OpenStreetMap forum (there is no Australia category) and the talk-au mailing list. Reply to messages within two working days while the event runs.
  • One hashtag, #MapManly2026, in changeset comments, with a link to the wiki page where the app allows it.
  • Everyone uses their own account; no shared or throwaway accounts, no misleading names.
  • Ground survey only: no copying from other maps; aerial imagery only from layers built into the editors.
  • Clean-up afterwards: the data lead reviews every changeset from the day within a week and fixes mistakes.
  • A short results write-up on the wiki page and the forum thread.

Other places the Australian community talks: Discord #oceania, Matrix #osmworld_oceania:matrix.org, and OSGeo Oceania (the local chapter). There’s also the Australian Tagging Guidelines for footpaths, which the field guide follows.

Measuring the result

Run the same measure before and after. scripts/coverage.py extends the routing spike’s coverage script to the town-centre box, with targets:

python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/coverage.py docs/mapping-day/data/osm-centre-{{osm_date}}.json
# a week after the day, once the data lead has finished:
python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/fetch_osm.py --label after
python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/coverage.py docs/mapping-day/data/osm-centre-{{osm_date}}.json docs/mapping-day/data/osm-centre-after-<date>.json

Roam Access targets (from the admin pages, not the script): a step-free entrance pin or a “no step-free way in” review for at least 60 town-centre venues, and arrival steps for at least 30.

Routing won’t change on the day. Edits appear on openstreetmap.org within minutes, but Roam Access routing (and any other router) only uses them after its next data refresh: the spike recommends rebuilding a self-hosted Valhalla weekly or nightly; hosted services such as OpenRouteService can be weeks behind. Check the routes again after the first refresh that includes the day’s edits.

Zone sheets A–F

Each sheet prints on its own pages: map first, then the checklist in walking order. A separate file with only the sheets is zone-sheets.html (PDF).

Files and data

  • data/tasks.geojson: every numbered point with its zone, type, what’s missing and its OpenStreetMap link. Open it in iD (Map Data → custom data), JOSM or geojson.io.
  • data/paths.geojson: footpaths missing surface, smoothness or width. data/zones.geojson: zone outlines, loops and meeting points. data/zones.json: everything the sheets are built from.
  • data/osm-centre-{{osm_date}}.json: the OpenStreetMap extract used (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL).
  • scripts/: fetch_osm.py (download), build_tasks.py (task list and zones), render_maps.sh + maps/render.html (zone maps), coverage.py (before and after), build_kit.py (this page and the sheets).

To rebuild everything with fresh data:

python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/fetch_osm.py
python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/build_tasks.py
sh docs/mapping-day/scripts/render_maps.sh        # Node 22+ and Google Chrome
python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/coverage.py docs/mapping-day/data/osm-centre-<date>.json
python3 -I docs/mapping-day/scripts/build_kit.py
sh docs/mapping-day/scripts/print_pdfs.sh
